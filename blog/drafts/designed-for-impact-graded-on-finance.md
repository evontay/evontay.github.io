# We designed for impact. The organisation graded us on finance.

*We designed a way to prioritise design work around impact, not budget. Except budget had already found its way in — quietly, through one question on the scorecard — long before the organisation made it official.*

---

Running a centralised, in-house design function comes with a specific kind of blindness. Requests arrive from everywhere — divisions, functional leads, teams you've never worked with — and almost none of them arrive with the context you'd need to know whether they actually matter. An embedded team has line of sight into what their business area cares about, because they're sitting inside it. A central team doesn't get that for free.

Before we had anything deliberate, the default sort was budget. Business units paid for our time out of their own budgets — a labour-credit model — so in practice, whoever had budget allocated for design work got served, whoever didn't waited, got told no, or stopped asking. Nobody had chosen that as a strategy. It was just what happens by default when there's no other sorting mechanism in the room.

So when we finally sat down to design something deliberate, budget wasn't the thing we were trying to fix. We weren't reacting to it at all — at the time, nobody on the design side knew that the organisation's own finances were a metric management would eventually judge us against. That pressure didn't exist yet, or if it did, nobody had told us. What we actually built the system around was a single question we believed in: where could design have the most impact on the company?

---

## Designing around impact, not access

Every request that reached discovery got evaluated against the same criteria, regardless of who was asking or what budget line they sat under: user and customer value, business impact, strategic fit, and on the other side, the risks — delivery risk, project risk, how legitimate and well-informed the request actually was. Findings got scored, then brought to leadership as comparable, structured evidence instead of whoever's meeting notes were most persuasive.

There was no line on the scorecard for budget, and nobody was asked directly whether they could pay. The closest it came was folded into strategic fit — does this align with where the organisation is already headed, does it have real backing behind it. Those questions can quietly correlate with funding without ever naming it; an initiative with strong organisational support usually also has a budget line, even when the scorecard never asked about the second thing. We didn't design that overlap on purpose. It's genuinely hard to separate "does the organisation care about this" from "has the organisation already put money behind it" — and to be fair, we didn't try very hard to, because that wasn't the fight we thought we were having.

Even with that soft spot, an early, unfunded initiative with a strong case could still outscore a funded one that had shown up too late to shape anything meaningful, because value and risk carried more weight in the total score than fit alone. Optimise mostly for where the work helps the company most, and a lot of the pay-to-play dynamics fall away — imperfectly, but for real.

It worked, more or less, for a while. Evaluation had an actual owner. A four-week discovery cap kept things moving. The framework itself kept getting recalibrated as we learned more — one of its criteria got swapped out entirely within the first six months, once enough live cases showed it wasn't predicting anything useful. It behaved like a living system, which is usually a good sign.

---

## The metric nobody told us about

Here's the part I'm still working through.

In hindsight, strategic fit was always the softest line on the scorecard — the one place a judgment call about backing and alignment could sit without ever being pinned to a number the way value or risk were. It hadn't mattered much yet, because nobody upstream was actively watching for it.

Years in, it became clear that management had its own scorecard for the design function, and finances were on it — how much of our time was being paid for, whether the group was pulling its weight in terms that could be defended upward. Nobody had designed our system with that pressure in mind, because nobody had told us it existed. It surfaced gradually, through budget conversations and headcount conversations that had nothing to do with our scoring framework at all — and by the time it was explicit, it was already shaping decisions.

Once "can they pay us" became something management was watching, it started creeping back into intake. Quietly, not as a rewritten policy — nobody added ability-to-pay as a scoring criterion. It happened more informally than that: projects that could pay simply got easier to say yes to than projects that merely scored well, and over enough decisions, budget was back in the room, just wearing better paperwork.

By the end, we'd arrived back at something close to the labour-credit default we started with. Not because the framework failed at what it was designed to do — it kept doing that fine — but because it was never designed to withstand a pressure nobody knew existed yet. You can build a system that's honest about impact. You can't build it to resist a metric the organisation hasn't told you it's using.

---

I keep coming back to whether this was specific to us, or just what happens to any shared service once the organisation starts asking it to justify its own existence in a language everyone upstairs understands, which is usually money. I suspect it's the latter, and I suspect most teams don't see it coming, the same way we didn't. You design around the thing you actually believe in — impact, in our case — and it can take years to find out what else the organisation was quietly measuring you against the whole time.

The honest resolution wasn't a better scoring framework. It was structural: moving more of the practice away from "central team evaluating requests from a distance" and toward something closer to the business itself, with enough shared stake that neither budget nor a hidden scorecard is the only lens either side has for deciding what's worth doing. That's a different story, and one I'm still in the middle of telling.
