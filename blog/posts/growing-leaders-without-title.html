On growing leaders without title

September 5, 2026
career development org design

This is a post related to the Designing Pods case study, where I will deep-dive into how I was thinking about the career development aspect of this pod model for team members involved.

Identity shifts under the pod model

Before any of the structural questions on the “how” of the pod model (how a pod would be staffed, how it would prioritise its own work, what it would be called), I wanted to carefully consider our team members’ experience into this new model of work. Even though there was no actual change in reporting lines, working in a pod is going to be a real transition for the team member, because this change is asking them to become a different kind of practitioner than the one they had trained to be.

Most of the team members stepping into pods had built their careers around depth in a single competency: research, design, facilitation, or whichever craft they’d spent years honing. What the pod model asked of them now was not just applying their specialised competency well, but integrating those skills into one coherent view of a problem in the domain they are in, and taking holistic ownership of its entire end-to-end experience.

This meant that instead of the relative safety of discrete, self-contained projects they are used to, they now have to deal with the messier, longer arc of a genuine strategy work, that doesn’t resolve in a sprint, and that doesn’t come with a clear finish line. They have to zoom out from a single moment in a user’s journey, to the full shape of that journey end-to-end.

For some team members, shifting to pod work also meant a shift from doing excellent hands-on craft to a different kind of work entirely: planning, communicating, facilitating, coaching. These are skills that hadn’t necessarily been part of what got them hired in the first place, but now is expected of them, framed as an opportunity to step up as “UX leaders”.

The “Pod rep” vs. “Pod lead” naming

In contrast to being the UX person in a product team, when you are working in a domain area pod with your UX peers, you need one person to hold it together internally and represent it externally - coordinating the team, and acting as the visible point of contact for senior stakeholders in the business area. This is where the transition I’ve just described stopped being abstract and became a real decision with real consequences for a real person.

Intentionally, I called the role a “pod rep”, even though “pod lead” was the more obvious term (and probably more honest of the role’s scope). But we were still at the stage of proving whether this whole way of working was viable at all, and pod members were often equal ranked peers from entirely different disciplines. Introducing a “lead” title among them, was risky not only because of the implied hierarchy of the term, but also that status among equals is a genuinely sensitive thing to disturb, especially inside an experiment nobody had yet agreed was permanent. “Rep” lets someone in the pod carry real representative responsibility without the label getting ahead of what the organisation had actually agreed to.

I also made a point of not choosing reps myself. Instead, I asked the managers of each pod’s prospective members to discuss among themselves who they thought would be a good fit. Partly this was practical: I didn’t have the standing to unilaterally assign a title to someone else’s direct report. But also because they are the people who had the most context on their reports’ actual readiness and career goals.

The cost of ambiguous authority

The tentative naming (”pod reps”) did let them step into real responsibility and start building the muscle of leadership without a premature fight over what that responsibility technically entitled them to. But it also meant the role’s authority stayed ambiguous for a while.

Some reps grew into the position with real confidence, using the exposure to senior stakeholders exactly as I’d hoped - presenting their pod’s work, building relationships at a level they wouldn’t otherwise have had access to, developing precisely the leadership muscle the role was meant to build. For others, the softness of the title made it harder to assert themselves when others pushed back on a decision, because nothing on their formal business title said they had standing above anyone else in the room.

Looking back, I think the caution that made “rep” the right call early on eventually became a limitation, once the model had proven itself and some individuals had genuinely outgrown the ambiguity. A name chosen to buy room for experimentation isn’t automatically the right name forever. Knowing when to revisit a deliberately soft decision, once the thing it was protecting no longer needs protecting, turned out to be its own judgement call.

An attempt to address to the Senior IC ceiling

Apart from all of the issues that this pod model was trying to solve for, there was a problem I really cared about: senior individual contributors, excellent at their craft, often hit a real career ceiling with no path forward without becoming a people manager. The management path is frequently not what they wanted, and not always where their strengths actually were.

The pod rep role, although imperfect and ambiguous at times, was my attempt to offer ICs an alternative: a real taste of leadership, real exposure to the stakes and stakeholders that come with it, without requiring anyone to abandon the craft identity they’d spent years building.

I don’t think I fully solved this. What I think I did was open a door that hadn’t existed before, one some people walked through with real success and others found harder to navigate than I’d hoped. Maybe what counts as progress depends on whether we let the door exist at all, rather than how gracefully everyone was able to walk through it.

Also, I’m still working out how to tell, in the future, the difference between a role that’s deliberately ambiguous because the moment calls for caution, and one that’s ambiguous because I haven’t yet done the harder work of deciding what it should actually be.

(P.S. In the end, that initial pod rep did get their promotion to Senior, based on their outstanding contributions proven during this pod experiment.)
