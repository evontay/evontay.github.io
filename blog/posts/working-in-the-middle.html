Working in the middle

September 11, 2026
middle manager alignment

There's a particular kind of disorientation that comes from sitting in the middle of an organisation — not at the top, not at the ground level, but somewhere in between — and realising that the people above you are also figuring things out. Not secretly. Not incompetently. Just... at their own pace, with their own incomplete picture, working toward a version of clarity that hasn't fully arrived yet.

I used to think misalignment happened because someone above wasn't communicating well enough, or because a strategy hadn't been articulated clearly enough for the layers below it to act on. And sometimes that's true. But more often, I've come to think it happens because every layer of an organisation is simultaneously mid-thought. Leadership is working out what they actually need. Middle layers are working out how to operationalise something that hasn't been fully defined. Ground-level teams are working out how to act on guidance that keeps shifting. None of this is dysfunction, exactly. It's just what organisations look like from the inside, at close range.

The tricky part is that these timelines never quite sync up. By the time clarity emerges at one level, the conditions at another have already moved. You can build something careful and well-reasoned — a framework, a process, a way of evaluating work — and find, years later, that the organisation had quietly developed its own criteria for judging you, criteria nobody thought to name out loud because they were still being worked out themselves. The gap isn't usually bad faith. It's just the ordinary lag between different layers of management thinking arriving at different conclusions at different speeds.

I don't think there's a clean solution to this, if you're sitting in the middle of it. Waiting for full alignment before acting means never acting. Ignoring the misalignment means eventually running into it at the worst possible moment. What I've found more useful — though it's an ongoing discipline rather than a fix — is something closer to continuous orientation. Not a one-time alignment check, but a regular habit of asking: has anything shifted upward? Downward? Sideways? Not anxiously, but attentively. The way you'd adjust your footing on terrain that's slightly uneven rather than flat.

The three directions matter roughly equally, even though the upward one tends to get the most attention. Checking sideways — whether peers and adjacent teams are navigating the same pressures or different ones — often surfaces the most useful early signals. Checking downward — whether what you're building is still serving the people it was meant to serve — keeps the work from drifting into something technically coherent but practically disconnected. Checking upward, of course, matters too. But it's rarely enough on its own, because the people above you are also mid-thought, and the picture they're forming may not be legible from where you're sitting until it's already arrived.

The honest conclusion isn't reassuring, but I've found it more useful than pretending otherwise: the alignment problem doesn't get solved. It gets managed, continuously, by people willing to keep looking in all three directions at once — and to hold the ambiguity of never quite knowing if they're fully oriented, while still moving forward anyway.
